The 81 squares before Sho-Time

Long before four MLB MVP awards, two World Series championships, and a 50–50 season, Shohei Ohtani was a high-school player staring at a sheet of boxes. The interesting part is not that the sheet predicted the future. It did not. The interesting part is how it translated an almost absurd ambition into things a teenager could practice that day.

September 23, 2026 · 12 min read · Goals, practice, Mandala

Shohei Ohtani’s story is often told backwards. We start with the impossible-looking results—the two-way seasons, the MVP trophies, the velocity, the home runs—and then search the past for a secret that explains them. That makes the famous goal chart from his high-school years easy to mythologize. A better reading is simpler: the chart was not magic, and it was not prophecy. It was a way to connect a distant ambition to a system of attention and behavior.

A sixteen-year-old and a goal that was almost too large

At Hanamaki Higashi High School in Iwate, Ohtani worked under coach Hiroshi Sasaki in an environment that treated planning as part of training. In the winter of his first year, around age sixteen, he filled out the goal sheet that would later circulate around the world. In the center he wrote a target commonly translated as “first-round draft selection by eight teams.”

It is an intentionally oversized target. A high-school player cannot command eight professional clubs to choose him. That is precisely why the surrounding cells matter. The center describes a desired outcome; the rest of the chart asks what kind of player—and person—would make that outcome more likely.

The eight pillars were not all baseball mechanics

The eight supporting areas are the part worth studying. Several are exactly what you would expect from an elite young pitcher: control, sharpness, 160 km/h velocity, breaking balls, and physical development. But three others widen the definition of preparation: mental strength, character, and luck.

That balance changes the nature of the plan. A normal training checklist might ask only how to throw harder. Ohtani’s chart also asked how to become more composed, more trustworthy, and more likely to create useful conditions around himself. Technical skill was necessary, but it was not treated as the entire system.

“Luck” became behavior

The most famous part of the chart sits under the category usually translated as luck or karma. Ohtani did not write “be lucky” and leave it there. The category was broken into behaviors such as greeting people, picking up trash, cleaning his room, respecting umpires, and becoming someone other people would want to support.

Whether you interpret those behaviors as character, social trust, discipline, attention, or an attempt to “attract luck” is less important than the structural move: something vague and uncontrollable was converted into actions under his control. You cannot schedule good fortune for 4:00 p.m. You can pick up the piece of trash in front of you.

That distinction is central to Mandala as a product. “Get 100 customers” is an outcome. “Contact ten qualified prospects with a concrete offer” is an action. “Become respected” is an outcome. “Keep the promise I made today” is behavior. The grid is useful when it keeps pushing language in that direction.

The chart did not come true exactly—and that makes the story better

The popular version of the Ohtani story sometimes implies that he wrote the goal and reality obediently followed the plan. It did not. In 2012, Ohtani publicly expressed a desire to go directly from high school to Major League Baseball. In the NPB draft, the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters still selected him in the first round. He was not literally chosen in the first round by eight Japanese clubs.

That is not a failure of the method. It is a reminder that a plan is a hypothesis, not a contract with the future. Ohtani’s path changed. The Fighters made a case that beginning in Japan could still lead to the Major Leagues and, crucially, offered him a route to continue as both a pitcher and a hitter. He signed and started his professional career in Japan in 2013.

This is one of the most important lessons hidden by motivational retellings: the center goal can change while the capabilities built around it remain valuable. Control still mattered. Strength still mattered. Mental steadiness still mattered. Character still mattered. The plan could be revised without making the years of practice meaningless.

From the grid to the two-way experiment

With Nippon-Ham, Ohtani developed the two-way role that many people had assumed would disappear once he became a professional. By 2016 he was the Pacific League MVP. He later moved to Major League Baseball and joined the Los Angeles Angels for the 2018 season, carrying the pitching-and-hitting experiment into a league where the idea initially looked even more improbable.

The results are now part of baseball history. Ohtani won the American League MVP in 2021 and again in 2023. After moving to the Los Angeles Dodgers, elbow rehabilitation kept him from pitching in 2024, so the shape of the challenge changed again. Instead of treating the lost pitching season as a blank year, he produced the first 50-home-run, 50-stolen-base season in MLB history, finishing with 54 home runs and 59 steals and winning the 2024 National League MVP unanimously.

By early 2026, Harvard Gazette could describe him as a four-time MLB MVP and a member of Dodgers teams that won the 2024 and 2025 World Series. None of those achievements was written in the center square of the high-school sheet. The path outran the original prediction.

So what did the 81 squares actually do?

They created a bridge between scales. The central ambition was measured in years. The outside cells could be practiced today. Between them sat eight intermediate areas that gave the ambition shape. That is the useful architecture:

Ambition → Drivers → Behaviors → Practice → Feedback → Revised plan

Without the middle layer, big goals often produce one of two failures. We stay abstract—“be great,” “grow the company,” “get fit”—or we jump straight into random activity. The chart forces a question between those extremes: what dimensions of the system must improve?

It also keeps the whole picture visible. Velocity sits next to character. Physical development sits next to mental strength. The format does not let one attractive subproblem silently become the whole goal.

What Mandala takes from the story—and what it does not

Mandala borrows the visual grammar of a center goal, eight surrounding drivers, and deeper action cells. But a software tool should not turn Ohtani’s handwritten sheet into dogma. Real goals are messier than templates. Some branches need deeper decomposition; others do not. Some generated actions should be deferred, delegated, or dropped entirely.

That is why the current product extends the grid with a decision layer: Break it down → Decide → Do next → Review. The 9×9 Grid preserves the classic overview on larger screens; the Map lets a branch recurse when needed; the Table makes decisions easier to scan; Review acknowledges that reality can invalidate the original plan.

There is another lesson in Ohtani’s draft story. A goal can be useful even when its literal wording does not happen. If the goal caused the right capabilities and habits to develop, it may have done its job. The plan should serve reality, not demand that reality serve the plan.

The part most people skip

It is tempting to copy the visible artifact—the 81 boxes—and miss the behavioral commitment underneath it. Filling a beautiful chart is easy. Practicing the unglamorous cells is the work. Picking up trash does not look like becoming an MVP. Cleaning a room does not look like throwing 160 km/h. Repeating those actions anyway is what turns a planning framework into a practice.

The strongest interpretation of Ohtani’s chart is therefore not “write down your dream and it will happen.” It is almost the opposite: write down the dream, then spend most of your attention on the small things you can actually control.

Where the story comes from

Terminology around the chart varies across English and Japanese sources—Mandala Chart, Mandal-Art, goal-achievement sheet, and Harada/Open Window 64 are sometimes used for overlapping visual forms. This article focuses on the documented Ohtani sheet and the planning pattern rather than claiming one definitive naming history.
